# How Croquet Came to Mill Lake — The Story of the Abbotsford Croquet Club

*This is the story of how croquet found a home on the lawns of Mill Lake Park
in Abbotsford, BC — and how a small Fraser Valley society, founded in Mission,
quietly became one of the mainland's newest croquet outposts.*
*(submitted by: Michael Kernaghan, Director, Fraser Valley Croquet Society)*

![Abbotsford Croquet Club at Mill Lake Park](https://abbotsfordcroquet.com/abby-croquet-cover.png)
*Mill Lake Park, Abbotsford — where the lawn bowlers learned to share.*

Croquet first arrived in the Fraser Valley by way of a familiar Canadian
arrangement: a few enthusiasts, a borrowed lawn, and the gentle suspicion that
the local lawn bowlers might not entirely understand what we were up to.

The Fraser Valley Croquet Society was registered as a BC non-profit in 2023
(S0075824), with its founding home in Mission. Like most clubs of recent
vintage, it began modestly — a handful of players, one set of mallets older
than at least two of them, and the long-standing Canadian conviction that
anything worth doing on grass is worth doing slowly.

## A Mainland Echo of the Victoria Model

Anyone who has read about Victoria's croquet scene will recognise what
happened next. The Island has spent more than a decade demonstrating that
lawn bowling clubs and croquet players can share a green without anyone
storming off in search of a rule book — and the same logic applies on the
mainland.

The Abbotsford Lawn Bowling Club, set in the gentle surroundings of Mill
Lake Park, agreed to welcome croquet onto its greens. The club was looking
for new members and an additional summer use for its lawns; the Fraser Valley
Croquet Society was looking for a home with a mowed surface, working
sprinklers, and adults who already understood the importance of flat shoes.
A partnership was duly struck.

Today, "Abbotsford Croquet Club" operates as the society's public-facing
brand at Mill Lake Park. The lawn bowlers continue their delicate
choreography on one rink while croquet players wage their slower, more
plotting campaigns on another. The two sports get along admirably,
provided no one mentions the difference between a roll-up and a draw shot.

## A Course in Mission, A Return to the Source

In 2026 the society is doing something it had been quietly planning for
some time: returning to Mission, the community where it all started. From
**April 20 to June 8**, the Mission Leisure Centre will host a six-week
*Introduction to Croquet for Adults and Seniors*, taught Monday evenings
in 90-minute sessions. No previous experience is required, and equipment
is provided — although enthusiasm, sensible footwear, and a willingness to
be gently corrected are encouraged.

The course is part recreation programming and part community outreach. It
is also, in a small way, a homecoming. The society's founding documents
list a Mission address; bringing the sport back to its place of origin —
this time as a public course rather than a private experiment — feels both
fitting and overdue.

## Mill Lake Play in 2026

Once the lawns at Mill Lake reopen for the season, regular play settles
into a familiar rhythm:

- **Tuesday and Thursday evenings, 5:30 PM** — May through September
- **Selected Saturdays** for Open Houses, club tournaments, and the kind
  of round-robin events that begin politely and end with someone insisting
  the wind moved their ball
- **Open Houses** in the spring, where newcomers can try the game with
  borrowed mallets and the patient encouragement of members who have
  forgotten how confusing the rules sound the first time

Equipment is provided, instruction is freely given, and the standard club
welcome applies: come once, stay for tea, return the following Tuesday
having quietly bought your own mallet from Oakley Woods.

## Why It Matters

The Fraser Valley sits in a happy middle ground — close enough to Victoria
to absorb its tournament culture, far enough from Vancouver to feel like its
own region. As croquet continues to expand across the Pacific Northwest,
having a registered society on the mainland gives Fraser Valley players
somewhere to belong, somewhere to enrol newcomers, and somewhere to point
when the inevitable question arises: *"Where can I actually play this?"*

For a sport that thrives on quiet civility and the occasional well-timed
roquet, that's a meaningful piece of infrastructure.
